# best tvs: how to narrow the shortlist by room and budget, and earn cashback on the set you buy

Type "best tvs" into a search bar and you get roughly the same eight or nine models across every serious publication. That's not laziness on the reviewers' part. It's genuinely a short list. What changes between articles is which set takes the top spot, and that usually comes down to whether the writer cares more about peak brightness or price.

What almost none of those lists cover is the second half of the transaction: the price you pay moves a lot more than the specs do, and the same panel can cost $600 less a few months later. Two things decide whether you get a good deal on a TV. Picking one that suits the room you actually own, and buying it through a path that pays something back. This article covers both, in that order.

## Start with the room, not the spec sheet

Most people shop backwards. They pick a model from a list, then discover it's too dim for a sun-drenched living room or absurdly oversized for a 7-foot viewing distance.

Four questions settle 80% of the decision:

- **How bright is the room during the hours you actually watch?** A room with a window opposite the sofa is a different product category than a basement. OLED sets have improved a lot here, but the anti-glare coatings and raw brightness of a good Mini LED still win in high-light rooms.
- **How far is your seat from the screen?** At 8 to 9 feet, 55 to 65 inches is comfortable for most people. If you're at 6 feet, a 65-inch panel gets tiring for anything that isn't sports or games.
- **Who sits off-centre?** OLED and QD-OLED hold their picture from the side. A lot of Mini LED panels wash out at the edges, which matters if four people share a sofa.
- **What are you connecting?** A PlayStation, an Xbox and a soundbar can eat three HDMI ports before you've plugged in a streaming stick. Count them.

That's the whole framework. Everything else is refinement.

> One thing worth knowing before you set a budget: What Hi-Fi? tested almost 50 TVs for its awards and still couldn't find a 32-inch set, or a budget 43-inch set, worth giving an award to. Small and cheap is the one corner of the market where the products genuinely aren't good yet.

## OLED, QD-OLED, Mini LED: what actually differs

The acronym soup is less complicated than it looks.

| Panel type | What it's good at | Where it falls short | Typical price position |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **OLED** | Perfect blacks, wide viewing angles, fast pixel response | Lower peak brightness in expensive models, burn-in risk with static content | Mid to premium |
| **QD-OLED** | OLED blacks plus much higher brightness and colour volume | Costs more, and stock of specific sizes is thin | Premium |
| **Mini LED (QLED)** | Very high brightness, no burn-in, strong value at large sizes | Blooming around bright objects on cheaper sets, narrower viewing angles | Budget to mid |
| **Standard LED** | Cheapest 4K option | No local dimming in most cases, weak contrast | Budget |

Mini LED is where the value sits. TCL's budget C7K beat Samsung, Sony and Hisense sets to take a What Hi-Fi? award for best 55 to 77-inch budget TV, and it also took best super-sized TV in its 98-inch form. The same publication handed the 50-inch TCL C6KS a win while noting it costs well under £500. Both are Mini LED.

OLED is still where the picture-quality ceiling is. What Hi-Fi? gave its Product of the Year and best premium 55 to 77-inch award to the Sony Bravia 8 II, a QD-OLED, and noted this was the first year a flagship OLED earned an award at all, because previous flagship premiums weren't worth the step up. Reviewers now argue the price-to-performance math at the top of the range has changed.

## Specs worth paying for, and specs that are noise

**Worth paying for:**

- **120Hz or higher refresh rate.** Not for gaming alone. Motion handling on sports and fast camera pans improves noticeably, and 60Hz panels are the single biggest tell on a cheap TV.
- **Two or more HDMI 2.1 ports.** You want at least one for a console and one spare. One is not enough.
- **Dolby Vision support.** If the set you're looking at skips it, you'll notice on streaming content mastered in it.
- **Measured brightness, not claimed brightness.** A 5,000-nit claim is only meaningful if independent testing backs it up.
- **Anti-glare coating.** In a room with windows, this single feature does more for perceived picture quality than a couple of hundred extra nits.

**Mostly noise:**

- **8K.** What Hi-Fi? dropped its 8K award entirely because there's still no meaningful 8K content and effectively one brand still making 8K sets in earnest. Skip it.
- **"AI processors" as a headline feature.** Every manufacturer has one. The useful question is whether the set handles upscaling and motion well in practice.
- **Contrast ratio numbers from the marketing sheet.** Native and dynamic contrast figures aren't comparable across brands.
- **Year-over-year model numbers.** CNET's position is blunt: TVs are stable technology and annual improvements are usually minor, so last year's model is often the smarter buy at roughly half the price.

## The price curve is where the real money is

TV pricing is not a straight line. It drops hard, spikes, then collapses again when the next generation ships.

A concrete example: the 65-inch LG C5 spent much of one fall retail window at **$1,399.99**, against a standard price Mashable put at **$2,699.99** for the same set. Same panel, same ports, nearly half the money.

That has a few implications:

1. **Set a target price before you shop.** Note the current price at two or three retailers. When a sale opens, compare against your own baseline, not the retailer's inflated "was" figure.
2. **Buy the model, not the season.** CNET's reviewers have argued the C5 was stuck awkwardly between a much cheaper previous-generation C4 and a much better G5, which is a pressure that eventually shows up as a discount.
3. **Know which windows are real.** Black Friday and Cyber Monday are genuinely the deepest discount window of the year for TVs, and doorbuster stock on specific models can sell out within hours. Waiting for the exact sale day usually costs more than buying the moment your target price appears.
4. **Consider last year's set deliberately.** If the C4 does 90% of what the C5 does for several hundred dollars less, that's not settling. That's the trade most people should make.

Being straight about the exclusions saves disappointment later.
